Station discovery · notebook 03

Delhi has 102 air monitors on OpenAQ.
Two rules cut them to 44.

Before any measurement is downloaded, one question has to be answered: which monitors are worth downloading? Two decisions answer it — D-003, a minimum history, and D-004, a recent window. This page is what came back, and what each rule threw away.

From 102 locations down to 44 selected stations

Each step here is a real filter in the notebook, not a rounding.

The trap on step one. The first request came back with exactly 100 locations — the page limit — and the metadata said there were more. Stopping there would have silently dropped two stations. The loop asks for page 2, page 3, and so on until a page returns fewer than 100.

Who runs the monitors

CPCB — India's Central Pollution Control Board — is the backbone: 106 of the 147 sensor records, and 42 of the 50 that qualify. The rest are state boards and a handful of low-cost sensors.

147 sensors, but only 102 locations. A station can hold more than one PM2.5 sensor — an old one that was retired and a new one that replaced it. Choosing one sensor per station, or stitching them, is part of what D-003 has to decide.

How much history each sensor really has

This is the check that was got wrong first, and then corrected.

clears the 730-day rule too short
the wrong way

Counting calendar years

The first version asked whether a sensor's first and last reading fell in different years.

A sensor running from 31 Dec 2025 to 1 Jan 2026 touches two calendar years and would have passed — on one day of data.

the fix

Counting elapsed days

coverage_days = (last_reading − first_reading).days, and keep it if it is 730 or more.

That measures the actual span. 50 sensors pass — at 50 distinct locations, so no station is double-counted.

What this check still does not prove. A span from 2016 to 2026 says the sensor reported at both ends. It says nothing about the gaps in between — dead months, missing hours, a stretch of zeros. That is DAF-08's job, and it is the reason the raw hourly data gets downloaded rather than the tidy /days aggregates.

The two rules, in order

The order matters. Quality first, then recency — that is how both decision records describe it.

accepted D-003

A station must have run for at least 730 days

coverage_days = (last_reading − first_reading).days, and keep it if that is 730 or more.

✓50 stations pass, at 50 distinct locations
✗95 sensor records fall short and are dropped

The record is honest about its own limits: “a minimum quality gate, not the final word”. It even recommends preferring the newest 3–4 years afterwards — which is exactly what D-004 does.

accepted D-004

Train only on the most recent four years

The window is computed from the latest available sensor date: 2022-09-16 → 2026-09-16, 1,462 days.

✓44 stations survive, saved to data/processed/valid_stations_recent_4y.csv
✗6 of the 50 have nothing inside the window at all

Delhi's traffic, fuel and policy have moved since 2016. A model that answers “what about tomorrow” should learn from the regime it is being asked about.

The two rules disagree more than the counts suggest. 50 → 44 looks like a gentle trim. It is not: D-003 rewards a long history and D-004 rewards a recent one, and at 37 of the surviving 44 stations that pair has kept a sensor which stopped reporting in October 2022 while discarding its live replacement.
