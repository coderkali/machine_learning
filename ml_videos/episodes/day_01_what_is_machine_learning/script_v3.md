# Day 1 — What is machine learning?

**Working title:** Stop writing rules. Show examples.  
**Audience:** Beginners, especially Java developers moving toward machine learning.  
**Runtime:** 88 seconds across seven separately directed narration beats.

## Voiceover and performance directions

Bracketed directions are ElevenLabs v3 audio tags, not spoken words. Ellipses cue short natural pauses. The recorded clips are stitched beat by beat.

### Beat 1 — 0–9s · The hook · curious and conversational

**Voiceover:** `[curious, conversational] Your inbox files a “you won a prize” email as spam. Nobody wrote a rule for it. ... How did it know?`

### Beat 2 — 9–21s · Why rules break · brisk, wry, then emphatic

**Voiceover:** `[matter-of-fact] Rule: prize means spam. [dry, wry] Real offers use that word too. [faster, exasperated] Add exceptions, and the rulebook becomes a maze. ... [slower, emphatic] Computers follow rules perfectly; people can’t anticipate every case.`

### Beat 3 — 21–31s · The change in approach · thoughtful and quieter

**Voiceover:** `[thoughtful, quieter] As the problem grew messier... a better question emerged... what if we showed computers examples... instead of spelling out every answer?`

### Beat 4 — 31–46s · Learning a pattern · teacher energy, crisp then flowing

**Voiceover:** `[instructive] Label emails spam or safe. [clear, building] The computer compares them, finds clues that tend to appear together, and adjusts its pattern from feedback. ... [deliberate] That learned pattern is a model. ... Training is how examples shape it.`

### Beat 5 — 46–59s · A new email · candid, then reassuring

**Voiceover:** `[quieter, candid] A new email arrives. ... The model checks its clues and predicts likely spam. ... [honest, softer] Prediction isn’t a promise. A strange new message can still fool it... [reassuring] so people stay in the loop.`

### Beat 6 — 59–74s · Java developer connection · direct and warm

**Voiceover:** `[warm, direct] For a Java developer, picture replacing a growing if-statement maze with two steps: train on labeled examples, then call predict for new input. ... [deliberate] Today, remember the shift; we’ll code it later.`

### Beat 7 — 74–88s · Takeaway and next step · confident, then inviting

**Voiceover:** `[confident] Machine learning learns from examples to predict new cases. Examples matter, and people still check results. ... [celebratory] Day 1 done! ... [curious] Next: what counts as data... and how do we load it in Java?`

## Visual direction

- Use concrete inbox examples on screen: “FREE PRIZE!!!”, “CLAIM CASH NOW”, a strange link, and ordinary messages such as a team lunch or invoice. Highlight the visible clues instead of using abstract dots alone.
- Carry the inbox world through every scene. Show the training model as a sorting machine that gets email examples wrong, receives a correction, and improves.
- Give the robot three readable reactions: facepalm at the growing rule maze, skeptical at “likely spam,” and a small nod when the model learns. Let it guide the viewer instead of sitting passively in a corner.
- Make each scene headline the dominant visual, then add information beneath it. Introduce a new visual detail at least every 2.5 seconds.
- End with the on-screen prompt: `COMMENT “MODEL” FOR THE DAY 1 CHEAT SHEET` and a Day 2 teaser.

## Production notes

- Caption from the spoken text only; do not display bracketed audio tags. Keep the `model.train` / `model.predict` screen example labeled “conceptual example”.
- Keep the prediction caveat clear: the model estimates; people review uncertain or consequential cases.
